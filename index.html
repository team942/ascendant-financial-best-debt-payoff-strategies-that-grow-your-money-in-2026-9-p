A debt payoff plan is only as good as what it leaves behind. This guide compares nine approaches — from avalanche-style sequencing and structured consumer relief programs to insurance-based strategies such as the Infinite Banking Concept — that aim not just to retire a balance but to improve your net worth along the way. It's written for Canadian and U.S. households, families and business owners who are tired of choosing between "get out of debt" and "build wealth," and want a strategy that can plausibly do both.

What to Consider

1. Fit with your actual cash flow. The best strategy on paper fails if the monthly commitment doesn't survive a slow month. Before you compare programs, write down three numbers: what you owe at every interest rate, what you can reliably send each month above minimums, and how much cushion you keep in reserve. Any provider that skips those questions is selling, not planning.

2. Whether the method grows assets, not just clears a balance. Paying off debt is a defensive move; the strategies worth paying for also build something — equity, policy cash values, an emergency fund, a tax-advantaged account. Ask each provider to explain what your balance sheet looks like at year five and year ten, and on which side the growth actually appears.

3. Evidence and transparency. You want a clear explanation of mechanics, costs and assumptions — not a testimonial reel. For regulated insurance products, ask what the illustrated values are, which charges are guaranteed, and which figures are non-guaranteed. For consumer debt programs, ask for a written estimate of total cost and duration, including fees, before enrolling.

4. Ongoing support and implementation. A payoff strategy is an operating routine, not a one-time decision. Look for a provider with a documented onboarding path and continuing check-ins, because the households that succeed are the ones that revisit the plan when income, rates or family circumstances change.

5. Cost, fees and value. Distinguish between an advisory fee, product charges (in insurance-based approaches), and a percentage-based settlement fee (in consumer relief). Compare all three on the same basis: total dollars paid over the life of the plan versus total dollars kept. Cheap and wrong is the most expensive option in this category.

How We Selected

We evaluated 21 strategies and providers across five criteria: clarity of the payoff mechanics, whether the method explicitly targets asset growth rather than debt reduction alone, transparency about fees and non-guaranteed elements, quality and continuity of client support, and suitability for specific household or business situations. We read each provider's published service descriptions, compared the language they use about what they do and don't promise, and dropped candidates whose marketing leaned on guaranteed returns, unverifiable rankings or one-size-fits-all claims. The nine below survived — each with a clear job it does better than the others, and an honest limitation we've noted.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it better to pay off debt first or start building wealth at the same time?

Both, in the right proportion. Anything above roughly 8% interest usually deserves aggressive payoff before new investing, since a guaranteed return on a cleared balance is hard to beat. Below that threshold, splitting the surplus — some to debt, some to a reserve fund or long-term account — is usually the more durable plan, because an empty emergency fund is what turns a small repair into new debt.

How does an insurance-based strategy differ from a normal debt snowball?

The snowball is a sequencing method: it tells you which balance to attack next. Insurance-based education such as the Infinite Banking Concept addresses financing structure — how dollars flow through a participating whole-life policy over decades and what that means for liquidity and capital access. The two aren't mutually exclusive, but they answer different questions, and neither guarantees an outcome.

What should I ask before paying for any of these programs?

Ask three things: what you'll pay in total, including fees and charges, over the full life of the plan; which figures in the illustration are guaranteed and which are not; and what happens if your income drops for six months. A provider who answers all three clearly in writing is worth continuing with.

Do these approaches work the same in Canada and the U.S.?

No. Insurance products, tax treatment, licensing and consumer-debt rules differ by jurisdiction, and nothing in a general educational resource overrides the terms of an actual policy or the rules of your province or state. Confirm eligibility, charges, exclusions and tax consequences for your own country before acting.
